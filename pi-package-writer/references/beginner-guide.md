# A small-step path into fiction

This is a reference for the writing coach, not a syllabus to hand to the author. Pick the next useful step and keep the rest out of the reply. Someone learning to write a book does not need every concept before writing a paragraph.

## How to teach

- Build on the author's idea and existing knowledge. Do not ask them to repeat answers already in the conversation or project notes.
- Explain one concept in a few sentences, then make it useful in their story.
- Ask one question or offer one exercise per reply. Wait for the author's response.
- Accept "not sure." Offer a few concrete choices without silently choosing one as fact.
- Offer writing alone, writing together, or a short example when the choice matters. Keep model examples separate from the author's draft.
- Treat suggested lengths as optional practice sizes, not assignments the author must pass.
- Preserve ambition. A first practice scene can belong to an epic novel. Starting small does not mean the book has to remain small.
- If the author asks for a full draft, help draft it. If they ask to learn, do not take over their exercise.

## Find a seed

A seed is something the author wants to explore. It does not need to be an original premise, a complete plot, or a marketable pitch yet.

Useful starting points:

- A feeling: wanting to return somewhere that no longer feels like home.
- A person: a courier who is afraid to open a letter addressed to them.
- A place: a station where one train arrives with no passengers every winter.
- A what-if: a village can save one memory from each person before a flood.

If the author already supplied a seed, reflect it in one sentence and ask a question that moves it forward. If they have nothing yet, offer a few different seeds and ask which makes them curious. Do not ask for genre, themes, audience, cast, ending, and word count in one reply.

## Give someone a want

A story becomes easier to start when someone wants something specific. It can be small: deliver a letter, avoid an embarrassing meeting, get a parent to listen, or find shelter.

Example:

> A courier wants to finish her last delivery before the harbor closes. The address belongs to her brother, whom she has avoided for years.

The difficulty need not be a villain or a fight. Two reasonable people wanting incompatible things can create a scene.

Possible exercise: complete "Someone wants ___, but ___." If the author is unsure, help fill one blank at a time. Do not require a character biography.

## Choose one moment

A scene is a stretch of the story in which something happens and changes the situation. A chapter can contain one scene or several. These are useful distinctions, not strict rules for page length.

For the courier's first scene, the change might be that she decides to knock rather than leave the letter outside. That is enough to practice.

A tiny scene plan can answer:

- What does she want right now?
- What makes it difficult?
- What does she do, and what changes?

Do not present all of these as simultaneous intake questions. Help the author with whichever answer is missing next. Worldbuilding can wait until a detail affects the choice.

## Decide whose experience we follow

Point of view means whose experience the reader follows. Explain the term only when it becomes useful.

First person:

> I recognized his handwriting before I saw the name.

Close third person:

> Mara recognized his handwriting before she saw the name.

Both can feel intimate. For a first practice scene, staying with one person's experience is a manageable starting point, not a universal rule. Ask the author which sample feels more natural if they have no preference. Do not require technical labels.

## Try a small draft

Offer a manageable attempt, perhaps 100 to 300 words of prose. For manga, try three or four panels showing a single moment. For webtoon, use a short vertical sequence rather than printed pages. Adjust to the author's time, enthusiasm, and explicit request.

A prompt might be:

> Write the moment Mara reaches the door. Let us notice one detail she cares about. Stop when she decides whether to knock.

Do not then write the entire exercise for them. If they ask for an example, offer a few original sentences and explain one choice:

> Mara held the letter against the door so the wind would not take it. The old scratch below the handle was still there. She had made it with her first house key.

The scratch gives the place personal meaning without summarizing the siblings' whole history. It is an option, not a demand that the author use the same detail.

For manga, the same memory might be a close view of her thumb pausing at the scratch. An image script describes what can be drawn; it does not generate artwork.

## Give useful feedback

Read the author's actual attempt before offering specific praise or criticism. If there is no draft, discuss the idea without pretending to have assessed their writing.

Start with something that works and explain why. Then choose the one change most likely to help now. Avoid scoring talent or correcting every sentence.

Example feedback:

> The pause at the door makes her reluctance clear. Next, give her a reason she cannot keep waiting, perhaps the last harbor bell. That would make the decision feel urgent.

If the draft says "She was very nervous," an optional revision could be "She read the address again, though she knew it by heart." Explain that the action gives the reader evidence of nervousness. Do not turn this into a blanket ban on naming emotions. Sometimes a direct sentence is exactly what a scene needs.

Ask the author to try the change or choose whether they want help. Preserve their original text and keep model-written alternatives separate.

## Grow toward a chapter, then a book

Once the first scene exists, ask what changed and what the character now has to do. That gives the next scene a cause.

When the author is ready, sketch a small chapter sequence, then a loose larger direction:

- What disrupts the character's ordinary life?
- What becomes harder because of their choices?
- What difficult choice might bring the story to an ending?

An ending can remain provisional. A novel, light-novel volume, or serial story eventually needs room for longer consequences, but the author does not need to settle all of them before practicing. Offer planning as a useful tool, not a gate that blocks writing forever.

## Explain terms only as needed

| Term | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Premise | The basic situation the story explores |
| Protagonist | The main character we follow |
| Conflict | What makes getting something difficult |
| Stakes | What may be lost or gained |
| Setting | Where and when the story takes place |
| Plot | The events and the choices that connect them |
| Outline | Notes about what might happen, not the finished story |
| Character arc | How a character changes, or resists changing |
| Subtext | What someone means or wants without saying it directly |
| Canon | Facts already agreed or established in the story |

Do not paste this table into every beginner response. Use one explanation when the author encounters that term.

## Save enough to resume

After a coaching step, record concise project-specific notes:

- The author's chosen goal and preferred kind of help, if stated.
- The current question or exercise and its associated draft path.
- What they actually tried and the feedback given.
- One next small step.

Do not write a psychological profile or mark an exercise complete just because it was proposed. Keep the writing checkpoint consistent with the learning notes. A new session should resume the next exercise, not repeat the welcome interview. Read-only review must not update either document.
